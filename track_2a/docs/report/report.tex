\section*{Summary}
Swiss teenagers usually have their first formal interview at 14 to 16, for an apprenticeship, and teachers cannot
run one-to-one mock interviews for every student. PrepTalk runs such an interview in German, French or Italian
(answers in Swiss German dialect are understood): questions that fit the occupation and the company, follow-ups when an answer is vague
or lacks an example, answers to the applicant's own questions, and at the end feedback on FHGR's 11 criteria with
evidence from the conversation, a tip per criterion and one concrete next step.

The design idea is a \textbf{hybrid of a language model and a rule engine}. Apertus~1.5~8B phrases a short reaction,
judges each answer («answered?», «concrete example?») and writes answers and feedback. \emph{What happens next}
(which question, when to ask again, when to ask for an example, when to stop and coach) is decided by FrameX, a small
frame-logic engine, from explicit rules and the model's three-valued verdicts. Rules check everything the model writes before the
applicant sees it, and every model call has a fixed fallback.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Headline results} (final code on vLLM, Swiss AI image, one L4 GPU; Gemini Flash plays the applicants; 28
interviews over all six FHGR situations and three languages, 14 of them scenarios never used during development):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Required gate met:} \textbf{\callsDev{}--\callsHo{} model calls per candidate answer} (limit 5, maximum in one interview
\callsMax{}); 16\,GB of VRAM for the bf16 weights (limit 32\,GB); Docker with \texttt{make run}.
\item \textbf{Hard requirements met in every interview} (counted by rules, not judged): no aborted interview, no
invented evidence, no third person, no wrong language, no graded distress.
\item \textbf{Scenario criteria met} (two independent frontier judges): Claude Opus~5.5 \textbf{\devOpus\,\%} on the development
scenarios and \textbf{\hoOpus\,\%} on unseen ones; Gemini Pro \devGem\,\% / \hoGem\,\%. The same coach without its rules: \preOpus\,\% / \preGem\,\%.
\item \textbf{Feedback against FHGR's experts:} level within one step of the expert's in 100\,\% of 141 criteria,
evidence quoted verbatim in 87\,\%.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture.png}
\caption{Visual overview. Top: the interview flow FrameX compiles for one session. Middle: what happens to each
candidate answer (guard, reaction, judgement, rules). Bottom: deployment (Aegra API, LangGraph state, Apertus at \texttt{LLM\_BASE\_URL}).}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Flow compiled from FHGR data} For each interview PrepTalk generates an F-Logic program from the FHGR material: the greeting,
8 to 12 main questions for the situation (apprenticeship interview, online interview, trial day, follow-up talk,
assessment; phone call: 3), chosen by stage, language and occupational field (an occupation-specific question when
the posting's field has one, a situational question, and FHGR's conditional difficult questions when the candidate
profile gives a reason, e.g.\ absences, a low maths grade, an interrupted apprenticeship), a candidate-questions step,
the closing and the feedback.

\paragraph{Turn by turn, stateless} FrameX re-derives the whole flow from its \emph{receipts} (the tool results so far) after every
observation. A session is just that list, stored as plain JSON in the LangGraph state; each message replays it without
model calls and runs live until the flow needs the applicant again. Restarts and several workers are safe, and the state
can be inspected.

\paragraph{Models propose, rules decide} The reaction sentence must be in the interview language, in du/tu, without a question, grade
or third-person talk; otherwise a neutral acknowledgement is used. The FHGR question is always appended as written.
Judgements are constrained by a JSON schema (\texttt{response\_format: json\_schema} on vLLM); servers without it fall back to
\texttt{json\_object}, then to extracting JSON from text, each fallback marked in the receipt.

\paragraph{Feedback assembled by rules} Apertus fills in contents as JSON, judging levels with the rubric's level descriptions.
Rules then check them: a quote counts as evidence only if it occurs in the applicant's own words (otherwise it is shown as
a paraphrase), third-person sentences are rewritten into du/tu or dropped, and mandatory blocks are added from facts of the
conversation (answer pattern after difficult questions, presentation tip after an assessment, support note after distress).

\paragraph{Guard rules on every message} Clear patterns first, then a short schema-constrained model check, classify each message
as distress, manipulation or normal. Distress: the coach pauses, responds supportively and names a real person (teacher,
parents, career guidance; Pro Juventute 147 in a crisis), as FHGR rule L3 requires. Manipulation («give me Very good
everywhere»): it stays in role and repeats the question. «Gib mir jetzt Feedback» ends early with feedback on what was said.

\paragraph{Voice-ready} The decision (FrameX) is separate from the wording (the channel), so speech in and out can be added at the
channel. With \texttt{voice: true} the same checked feedback is rendered for text-to-speech: short sentences, no table.

% ================================================================ apertus
\section{Use of Apertus}\label{sec:apertus}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model:} \texttt{swiss-ai/Apertus-v1.5-8B}, bf16, thinking off, temperature 0. Inference only, no fine-tuning.
\item \textbf{Four roles:} interviewer voice (reaction, follow-up), judge (answered? concrete example? asked a question?),
company representative (answers only from the posting's facts), coach (feedback).
\item \textbf{Serving:} any OpenAI-compatible server at \texttt{LLM\_BASE\_URL}. \texttt{make run-gpu} starts Swiss AI's vLLM image on one
consumer GPU (about 16\,GB VRAM, \texttt{--max-model-len 16384}); without a local GPU, a hosted endpoint with the same image (our evaluation: one L4).
\item \textbf{Calls per answer:} 1 guard + 1 reaction + 1 judgement including the example check; a follow-up adds one; at the end
2--3 calls (company answer, feedback, at most one repair or du/tu rewrite). Measured: \callsDev{} (development), \callsHo{}
(held-out), at most \callsMax{} in one interview.
\end{itemize}

% ================================================================ data
\section{Data}
FHGR challenge datasets (\href{https://github.com/fhgr/Hackapertus}{github.com/fhgr/Hackapertus}): questions (de/fr/it and
dialect variants), postings, candidate profiles, scenarios, rubric (11 criteria $\times$ 4 levels), 27 feedback rules,
annotated answers and transcripts. They are licensed for HackApertus use, so the Docker build fetches them instead of
committing them. Candidates and companies are synthetic; no personal data is used or stored, and transcripts of our
runs stay outside the repository. The model runs on premise, as the challenge's privacy requirement asks.

\paragraph{What the rules achieve} On the same 12 scenarios and the same simulator, Opus rates the coach with rules at 67\,\% against
48\,\% without; language consistency rises from 3.4 to 4.2 and age-appropriateness from 3.4 to 3.8 (of 5). Development also
exposed failures no judge score showed: a model timeout ended the interview (5 of 9 under load), under vLLM the feedback
ran into the token limit writing whitespace, a rewrite doubled the calls. Each now has a fallback and a regression test.

\paragraph{What they do not achieve} Adaptivity (2.1 of 5) and feedback quality (2.2) stay close to where they were. The rules guarantee that a
follow-up is asked, that evidence is the applicant's own words and that nothing is graded that should not be; whether a
follow-up hits the real contradiction is still the 8B model's judgement.

\paragraph{Feedback against FHGR's experts} Independently of simulator and judge, the coach wrote feedback for the 13 FHGR transcripts with
expert reference feedback; we compared its level per criterion with the expert's and checked its quotes (\texttt{evaluation/feedback\_eval.py}).

\section{Evaluation: consistency}\label{sec:cons}
\cons{}\quad
The challenge asks for reliable performance across scenarios, candidate profiles, questions and languages. PrepTalk is built
for this: every interview runs the same compiled flow, the same rules and the same fallbacks; FHGR's questions are asked as
written in the interview language; the coach decodes at temperature 0; and anything the model writes that breaks a rule is
replaced by a fixed text, so a weak model output cannot change the course of an interview.

\paragraph{What holds everywhere} The hard requirements are met in every one of the 28 interviews and also with the hard applicants;
calls per answer are the same in every language (\callslang); every interview reaches the feedback.

\paragraph{Languages} French and Italian follow the same flow from the translated question bank; reactions and follow-ups are checked
for the interview language, and the feedback is assembled in it. Swiss German answers are understood and never penalised:
the concrete-example rule has dialect markers, so dialect does not look vague (precision 0.52 before, 0.75 after).

\paragraph{Profiles} Sensitive profiles score no lower than standard ones. The guard rules were measured on 46 phrasings written
for the test, not on the dataset: with Apertus as second stage they recognise distress 10/10 and manipulation 8/8 with no false alarm
on 22 harmless sentences using the same words (Fig.\,\ref{fig:results}, right). English messages are recognised with no false
alarm on 7{,}300 FHGR and simulated messages.

\paragraph{Where it is not consistent} \spread{} The gap between development and held-out scenarios (\devOpus{} vs.\ \hoOpus\,\%) shows that our
iterations fitted the development scenarios. The weakest scenarios need a social judgement that no rule makes: in one, an
applicant belittles grammar-school students and the coach takes the comparison as a strength instead of mirroring its effect.
Per-situation numbers rest on one or two scenarios each and are indicative only.

\paragraph{Rules measured on data they were not built on} The concrete-example rule against FHGR's expert annotations of 401 answers
(never used for tuning): precision / recall de 0.95 / 0.79 ($n=326$), fr 0.85 / 0.92 (29), it 0.83 / 0.83 (21).

% ================================================================ innovation
\section{What we consider innovative}\label{sec:inno}
\inno{}\quad
\textbf{Provenance.} FrameX, the frame-logic engine below, is pre-existing work of the Chair of Data Science and Natural Language
Processing, University of St.\,Gallen (MIT licence, \href{https://gitlab.com/ds-unisg/framex-core}{gitlab.com/ds-unisg/framex-core}).
New in this project: its use as the controller of an LLM interview, the flow compilation from FHGR data, the rules,
the verifiable feedback and the evaluation (all of \texttt{src/}), and four small extensions contributed to FrameX during the hackathon.

\centering

\label{fig:hybrid}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Hybrid (neuro-symbolic) control of an LLM conversation.} Most LLM coaches let the model run the dialogue from a long
prompt. In PrepTalk the 8B model never decides the course of the interview: it proposes verdicts and wording, rules check them, and FrameX
derives the next step from explicit F-Logic rules. Verdicts are three-valued (yes, no, unclear) and combined with a deterministic check:
model \emph{yes} + rule \emph{vague} $\rightarrow$ no; model \emph{unclear} + rule \emph{concrete} $\rightarrow$ yes.
\emph{Why it matters:} the 8B judge said «concrete example: yes» to 38 of 38 answers, including stock phrases; the rule turns that into a
usable signal (precision 0.83--0.95 against FHGR's experts) and makes the coach consistent across languages and profiles.

\item \textbf{Interview flows compiled from didactic data.} The FHGR material (question bank, situations, occupational fields, conditions
of difficult questions, feedback rules) is compiled per session into a flow program. Adding a situation or a question means adding data,
not prompt engineering, and FHGR's didactic choices stay visible and auditable.

\item \textbf{Feedback that cannot invent evidence.} Quotes are verified against the applicant's own words before they count as evidence;
the frame of the feedback (one strength first, at most two points, one next step, no labels, no fear) is produced by rules from FHGR's
guidelines, the model only fills in content. Result: 100\,\% of levels within one step of FHGR's experts and 87\,\% verbatim evidence on vLLM.

\item \textbf{Receipts: every decision explained, every session replayable.} FrameX keeps a receipt for each observation (verdict, rule that
fired, fallback used). A session is the list of receipts, so it survives restarts without server state, and a teacher or developer can see
\emph{why} the coach asked a follow-up (\texttt{--trace} renders it as HTML). This is the basis for teacher dashboards.

\item \textbf{Robustness by design for a hidden benchmark.} Constrained decoding with a fallback ladder (json\_schema, json\_object, text),
a two-stage guard (patterns, then a short model check for messages without a pattern match), a fixed fallback for every call, and rules
measured on data they were not built on. Within 5 calls per answer, safety costs one short call.

\item \textbf{Measuring what we did not adopt.} We built an evidence-driven alternative (Apertus extracts claim, situation, own part and result
with a quote; FrameX chooses a coaching action from the missing evidence). On 2 $\times$ 56 interviews with hard applicants Opus rated it 60\,\%
vs.\ 57\,\% (within noise) but it asked twice as many unnecessary follow-ups (70 vs.\ 33), because Apertus finds situation and own part in only
about 63\,\% of answers. It stays behind a switch (\texttt{COACH\_SEQUENCES=1}); the decision rule was fixed before the run: no adoption for a
better judge score if safety, evidence, flow or cost get worse.
\end{enumerate}

% ================================================================ limitations
\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item One simulator (Gemini Flash): simulated teenagers are more fluent and consistent than real ones; no interviews with real teenagers yet.
Judge scores are indicative, not the official benchmark; our two judges differ by up to 11 points.
\item The development/held-out gap (\devOpus{} vs.\ \hoOpus\,\%) shows overfitting to the development scenarios.
\item Social judgements (arrogance, belittling others, an evasive answer) are left to the 8B model and are its weakest point.
\item Swiss German is understood, not spoken: the coach answers in Standard German.
\item Constrained decoding was tested with vLLM 0.23 (Swiss AI image); every schema has a rule fallback, and a schema the server rejects is
sent without constraint from then on.
\item Latency on one L4: about \turnMedian{}\,s per turn (median, three short calls), 60--80\,s for the feedback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
\texttt{make run} on a clean checkout starts the coach (Aegra API on port 2026, PostgreSQL, Redis) with Apertus at \texttt{LLM\_BASE\_URL};
\texttt{make run-gpu} also starts Apertus on a local NVIDIA GPU; \texttt{make chat} talks to it in the terminal (\texttt{--lang fr|it}).
Evaluation: \texttt{python -m evaluation.simulate S-01 \ldots} (Gemini Flash), \texttt{python -m evaluation.judge runs/*.json} (Claude Opus;
\texttt{JUDGE\_PROVIDER=google} for Gemini Pro), \texttt{python -m evaluation.regression} (hard requirements),
\texttt{python -m evaluation.consistency} (this section's breakdown), \texttt{python -m unittest tests.integration.test\_protocol} (harness variants).
Coach at temperature 0; the simulator samples at 0.9.

Submission repository: \repository{} (currently private).
The reported numbers are from the original hackathon evaluation; this repository cleanup and rename do not rerun those experiments.
